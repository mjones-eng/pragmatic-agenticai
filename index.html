Agentic AI Could Bring Software’s Profits Forward by Years

There is a link between stock prices and agentic AI that Wall Street is overlooking. Agentic AI could make a handful of software companies substantially more profitable, and investors who own them early stand to benefit.

Wall Street’s attention is fixed on Anthropic’s initial public offering, OpenAI’s compute agreements, Nvidia’s competitive position and the trillions of dollars going into AI infrastructure. There is money to be made there.

The debate that surrounds it asks whether AI is a bubble, whether corporate AI projects are failing, and whether the financing behind the infrastructure boom makes sense. Far fewer investors ask where the money goes after AI reaches the businesses that use it. Hardly anyone traces that path from a company’s operations, through its income statement, and into the dollars added to its share price.

Tracing it comes down to a simpler question than any in that debate: who will make more money because of AI, how much more, and how soon?

We traced it through actual businesses, and the opportunity at the end of that path is large. In our experience as contrarian investors, the link between agentic AI and share prices is worth a 2x to 4x return, and more if the market is in a good mood.

No reader should accept that on our word, and no reader has to, because the money can be followed. This insight follows it in three steps: the opportunity, the way it turns into earnings and share-price appreciation, and a short list of companies positioned to benefit. The numbers can then be judged directly.

The idea
Agentic AI sets off a domino effect inside a certain kind of software business. A single change in how engineers work travels through the income statement and into the share price, and it brings forward by years the profits the market expects much later.

1Change is coming

As agentic AI spreads, we expect the stocks of one kind of software company to multiply in value. These companies share three characteristics:

  1. High revenue growth.
  2. Heavy spending on research and development (R&D) relative to revenue.
  3. Little or no profit.

Together, they describe a business that is growing quickly and spending heavily to keep growing, but keeps very little of the revenue it collects.

Agentic AI could change that. These companies could keep growing quickly while retaining far more of each revenue dollar, so a business that earns almost nothing today could become highly profitable without giving up growth.

If that happens, the market’s expectations for these businesses will be overturned. Wall Street’s earnings forecasts will become obsolete, analysts will have to rebuild their models, and investors will have to reconsider what these businesses are worth.

The headlines that follow are easy to imagine:

“Figma stock doubles as agentic AI lifts margins and sends earnings soaring.”

“Analysts raise Rubrik price target 150% as coding agents transform free cash flow.”

Those headlines, and the analyst notes and investor commentary around them, will describe the change as a surprise. It will not be one. It will be the predictable result of a domino effect set off by agentic AI. Because that result can be foreseen, investors can own these businesses before Wall Street recognizes the change, and before the largest gains have been made.

2The domino effect

The first domino is engineering productivity. Coding agents, the form of agentic AI built for software development, multiply what each engineer can produce.

With coding agents such as Claude Code, Codex and Cursor, engineers produce more working code in less time, which is why most software developers now use them. Three examples from very different companies show how large and widespread the gain is:[1]

Engineering capacity on that scale can be remarkably inexpensive to add. One 2025 study, known as SWE-Lancer, compared what an AI model cost to run with what developers had actually been paid for the same software work. OpenAI’s o1 model completed $20 of that work for every $1 it cost to run, even after counting the attempts that failed. And o1 is now an older model. Today’s coding agents pair far stronger models with more sophisticated workflows, so we expect the return on that spending to be higher still.[2]

Measured against payroll, the cost is just as modest. Anthropic reports that companies using Claude Code pay $150 to $250 per developer per month on average. That is roughly 1% to 2% of what a software-as-a-service (SaaS) company pays a software developer. For that small addition to payroll, management makes the engineers it already employs far more productive.

Far more output from the same engineers, at little added cost, is more than a change in how software gets written. It is AI’s Trojan horse: it enters a company as a tool for engineers and ends up changing the economics of the business. That is where the opportunity for investors lies.

The economics change because, in a SaaS business, three things move together: engineering headcount, the product roadmap and revenue growth. More engineers build more products, and more products bring in more revenue. Until now, doubling the business has required a large expansion of the engineering team.

Agentic AI is breaking that link. A company no longer needs the same number of engineers to reach the next level of scale, so it can keep growing revenue while adding far fewer engineers than it had planned.

None of this requires layoffs. The company simply hires far fewer engineers than it otherwise would. That avoided spending can be worth billions of dollars in market value, because revenue keeps coming in while less of it goes back out.

For a high-growth software company that barely turns a profit, keeping more of each revenue dollar makes an enormous difference: profit margins expand, earnings grow much faster than revenue, and earnings per share rise sharply.

The pattern itself is standard software economics. A company spends heavily to grow until revenue starts to outpace expenses, and then profits rise quickly. Agentic AI could bring that turning point forward by years, and the market’s expectations do not yet reflect it.

When they do, earnings forecasts will rise, valuations will be reconsidered, and these stocks could be revalued substantially.

Within a few years, this may simply be how software businesses operate, and higher earnings will surprise no one. By then, the largest gains may already have been made. The opportunity lies in owning these businesses before Wall Street catches up.

3A simple thought experiment

A simple example with round numbers makes the effect concrete.

Consider a software company with $1 billion in annual revenue. It spends $300 million on R&D, and after all other costs it keeps $30 million in operating profit. Of every dollar that comes in, three cents is left over.

Next, double the business. At 20% to 25% annual revenue growth, revenue reaches $2 billion in roughly three to four years.

Software businesses already benefit from operating leverage, meaning their costs tend to grow more slowly than their revenue. We therefore assume R&D spending grows 85% while revenue doubles, and we hold all other costs at the same share of revenue. R&D reaches $555 million and operating profit reaches $105 million. That is a good result: revenue doubles and operating profit more than triples.

Now introduce coding agents. Suppose management delivers the same revenue growth while increasing R&D spending by 50% instead of 85%.

The business is still investing heavily in product development: R&D spending rises by $150 million. Management simply avoids the additional $105 million it would have spent on the baseline path.

That $105 million becomes operating profit, on the same revenue and the same growth. Revenue doubles, and operating profit rises sevenfold. Exhibit 2 lets you set the R&D growth rate yourself.

4The domino effect continues

The step-change in R&D efficiency gives shareholders two further advantages: faster revenue growth and a higher ceiling on profit margins.

Coding agents do more than help software companies earn more on the revenue they have. They can also help them grow faster.

A large part of revenue growth depends on how much a business can spend on sales and marketing, because even a strong product needs sales and marketing to reach customers. Resources are limited, and money spent developing products cannot be spent selling them. Management therefore has to divide its budget between sales and development.

Coding agents change that trade-off. As engineering becomes more efficient, management can redirect some of the savings toward expanding the sales force and acquiring more customers, which sets off a second domino effect.

More salespeople bring in more customers, and more customers accelerate revenue growth. That additional revenue then flows into a business with better profit margins, so faster revenue growth and higher margins combine into even faster earnings growth.

Shareholders benefit twice. They earn more on the growth the business would have had anyway, and they may also own a business whose growth accelerates. Earnings grow larger and arrive sooner.

One budget, two usesEvery dollar saved on development can go to profit or to sales. Management decides the split.

The second advantage goes deeper, to the underlying economics of software. Agentic AI could change how profitable a software business can ultimately become.

Historically, mature software businesses have spent more than 15% of revenue on R&D. For every dollar of revenue, at least 15 cents went back into developing products. Agentic AI could change that permanently.

Coding agents could bring that figure down to 10 cents, to 7.5 cents, or even to 5 cents.

Applied to billions of dollars in annual revenue, those savings would be an enormous windfall for shareholders. Headcount could keep growing in absolute terms, but its old relationship with revenue would be broken.

With agentic AI, the ceiling on software profit margins would be much higher, and a higher ceiling can raise the multiple of earnings the market is willing to pay for these businesses.

5What it could mean for the share price

In the end, what matters to an investor is how much the share price rises.

We think about valuation as a company’s earnings multiplied by the price the market is likely to pay for each dollar of them, known as the price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple. The aim is direction more than precision: an estimate of a sensible share price under reasonable conditions. We prefer a defensible fair value to a “precise” valuation every time.

In the doubling example with coding agents, operating profit rises from $30 million to $210 million. Assume no net interest expense, a constant 25% tax rate and 100 million diluted shares. Earnings per share rise from about $0.23 to $1.58.

Start the stock at 100 times its current earnings, which puts the initial share price at $22.50. A P/E of 100x assumes the market already expects substantial earnings growth and operating leverage.

As that profit arrives, the multiple often compresses materially. Our base case cuts the P/E in half, from 100x to 50x, over the same period in which revenue doubles.

Earnings per share grow sevenfold while the multiple halves, so the modeled share price reaches $78.75. A hypothetical $100,000 position becomes $350,000.

A dramatically better earnings trajectory is also likely to attract investor enthusiasm. If the P/E keeps three-quarters of its starting level and ends at 75x, the position becomes $525,000. If enthusiasm holds the multiple at 100x, it becomes $700,000.

7Why “SaaS is dead” is the wrong conclusion

One last point concerns the narrative that “SaaS is dead.” We do not accept it. A successful SaaS business is much more than its code. If code were the only moat, these companies would not spend 50% more on sales and marketing than they do on R&D.

That selling effort also teaches them what customers actually want, an insight a new competitor cannot simply copy.

They have also spent years building specialized products, integrating themselves into their customers’ operations, and earning the trust of companies that depend on their software for mission-critical work. An AI startup will not displace them simply because writing code has become cheaper.

We see little sign that the frontier AI labs want to become specialized SaaS businesses. Nor are customers better off building and maintaining the software themselves, because they lack the domain specialization that makes agentic AI work in practice.

Customers also have easier savings available. Using agentic AI to augment their own workforces would save them far more than cutting SaaS spending, and companies are already moving in that direction this year (Exhibit 8). [4]

The business model will change, from charging per seat to charging for usage and, increasingly, for outcomes. That shift is not necessarily negative. When pricing is tied to outcomes, the vendor shares in the value its products create instead of collecting a flat fee, which can make SaaS economics more attractive. Enterprises and SaaS vendors could come to work more like partners.

Pragmatic perspective

The companies Wall Street fears AI will destroy are among those putting it to work.

What we find most interesting is that these companies are not waiting to be disrupted.

We see far more opportunities for agentic AI to increase their earnings than the prevailing narrative suggests.

An investor who lets that narrative guide the search will overlook stocks with the power to transform a portfolio.

What to remember

  1. The stocks we expect to multiply belong to software companies with high revenue growth, heavy R&D spending relative to revenue, and little or no profit.
  2. Coding agents break the link between engineering headcount and revenue growth. The savings come from hiring fewer new engineers, not from laying off existing ones.
  3. In the thought experiment, revenue doubles and operating profit rises sevenfold. The domino effect then continues into faster growth and a higher ceiling on software profit margins.
  4. Even with the P/E cut in half, a hypothetical $100,000 position becomes $350,000. The opportunity lies in owning these businesses before Wall Street catches up.
  5. “SaaS is dead” overlooks what these businesses are: specialized products, deep customer relationships and sales engines that cheaper code does not replace.
